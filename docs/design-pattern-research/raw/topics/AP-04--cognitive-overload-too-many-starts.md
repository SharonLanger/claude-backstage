# AP-04 Cognitive overload / too many starts

## When it comes up

- “I send off a quick task whenever I have a spare minute. Now all the results need me at once.”
- “I started three changes while reviewing the first one, and the next reviewer has all four waiting.”
- “Each task feels easier, and I still end the day with more unfinished work.”

The brief connects a lower barrier to starting with overload for individuals and teams. The accepted evidence shows how work expanded at a specific company and how some workers experienced the change. Team delivery, maximum useful concurrency, and the consequences of a growing review queue are separate questions.

## What the field evidence establishes

In a Haas News interview, investigator Xingqi Maggie Ye describes eight months of ethnographic fieldwork at a U.S. technology company of about 200 employees. She reports regular workplace observation and more than 40 semi-structured interviews across functions. The interview count does not enumerate unique participants. The account records tasks that staff became willing to take on, work fitting into formerly idle intervals, and AI processes running alongside meetings, drafting, or code review. Some staff ran several agents. A background process can overlap review while the person remains focused on one task. The example establishes overlapping activity and leaves attention-switching costs unknown. `SRC-0015:C01`, `SRC-0015:C02`, `SRC-0015:C03`, `SRC-0015:C04`, `SRC-0015:C05`. [Dossier](../sources/SRC-0015--work-intensification-and-task-expansion.md) · [Haas News interview, lead and answers 1–3](https://newsroom.haas.berkeley.edu/ai-promised-to-free-up-workers-time-uc-berkeley-haas-researchers-found-the-opposite/)

Some workers characterized brief AI interactions as energizing, while their accounts of the workday as a whole included pressure or difficulty disengaging. Local momentum and cumulative strain can coexist. Ye’s proposals that expectations rise with expanded capacity and that repeated switching can harm judgment are interpretations arising from the case. The account gives no comparison group, direct cognitive-load measure, subgroup rates for software developers, or change in team completion time, defects, or review capacity. The University of California repost and the investigators’ earlier *Harvard Business Review* article trace to the same study. The accepted dossier uses the original university interview and the HBR public preview for provenance. Independent evidence on the same concurrency mechanism is still needed. `SRC-0015:C02`, `SRC-0015:C06`, `SRC-0015:C07`. ## Practical boundary

Ye proposes collecting non-urgent updates at planned times, protecting focused work, and preserving time for colleagues to compare observations. A coding team could use the account to ask where completions actually queue and how much review the team can absorb. A trial that batches routine completions and measures time from start to verified delivery, number of open changes, and time spent reviewing would be a local adaptation. The interview reports no trial of those practices, throughput limit, or work-in-progress cap. There is no accepted catalog pattern for AP-04. `SRC-0015:C08`.
