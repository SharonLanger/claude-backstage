# Measuring AI Ability to Complete Long Software Tasks

## Provenance and scope read

Thomas Kwa and Ben West share lead credit on this METR benchmark study. The first preprint appeared on March 18, 2025, the study appeared at NeurIPS 2025, and arXiv v4 is dated July 10, 2026. The author list above follows the v4 record and the conference PDF, including Chris Painter. The conference landing-page byline omits Painter, while the v4 record explicitly notes the correction. The arXiv abstract page still gives Claude 3.7 Sonnet as an example at roughly 50 minutes. Both the v4 full-text abstract and the conference paper identify o3 at roughly 110 minutes. Results below follow the full texts. METR assembled and ran the benchmark as part of its AI capability evaluation work. The paper lists two affiliation footnotes for work performed at METR. ## Reader's orientation

Across this set of autonomous software and research benchmarks, models generally succeeded less often on tasks that took skilled humans longer. A model's fitted time horizon moves with the reliability required. The human reference group and the makeup of the task suite both influence the estimate. The paper offers examples of repeated failures and errors introduced during attempted repair, and finds that more complicated operating conditions can predict lower success even after accounting for human time. For OB-08, the strongest evidence is the association between benchmark completion and duration. The causal contribution of any one kind of difficulty, the rate of cascading errors and the effect of handing the same work to an agent in smaller pieces remain open. [ArXiv v4, §§2.3–4, Fig. 3 and Appendices D and F.2](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.14499v4)

## Detailed analytical reading

### What took longer, and for whom

The experiments combine 97 HCAST tasks spanning roughly one minute to 30 hours, seven eight-hour RE-Bench research engineering tasks, and 66 Software Atomic Actions (SWAA) that take seconds. Table 1 illustrates how the work differs across the range. Picking out a shell script by its name is a seconds-long SWAA item. Inferring a JSON conversion from examples is a roughly 56-minute task, and implementing a faster GPU backtesting routine with equivalent behavior is an eight-hour example. Longer and shorter items can differ in knowledge, task format, tools, and the need to integrate intermediate results. The introduction and Figure 2 give 170 tasks, matching the three listed suite counts, while §2.2 uses 169 when reporting human baseline coverage. The paper reports more than 800 human attempts totaling 2,529 hours and says 148 tasks received human baselines and 21 HCAST tasks used researcher estimates. [ArXiv v4, Fig. 2, Table 1, §§2.1–2.2 and Appendix B.1](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.14499v4) · [NeurIPS paper, pp. 3–4](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2025/file/85069585133c4c168c865e65d72e9775-Paper-Conference.pdf#page=3)

For timed tasks with successful runs, the reference duration comes from their geometric mean. The HCAST and RE-Bench baseliners were external contractors with relevant expertise. SWAA used internal staff and a tighter timing protocol designed to isolate the time to read and answer a single item. RE-Bench instead assigned a fixed eight-hour reference, with the success threshold derived from average human scores from seven-to-nine-hour attempts. On HCAST, the successful-human and successful-agent cutoffs used the same task-specific target. Conditioning on successful human runs can favor shorter durations, a concern the authors emphasize for harder tasks with fewer human successes. In a small annotated sample, reading directions and figuring out submission consumed a substantial share of human time on the short tasks. A task duration can therefore reflect benchmark overhead and context acquisition as well as the underlying software work. [ArXiv v4, §3.1 and Appendix C.1.1–C.1.5](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.14499v4)

### Frequency of completion across tasks

The authors evaluated agents built on 12 frontier and four near-frontier model versions from 2019 to early 2025, with approximately eight attempts per evaluated agent-task combination. The systems included the model and its scaffold, with model-specific differences in some scaffolds and elicitation and simpler prompting for SWAA. GPT-2 was incompatible with the scaffold and assigned zero on HCAST and RE-Bench. Main-body results down-weighted tasks from large families by the inverse square root of family size. Figure 3 plots the negative association between log human duration and average agent success across the tested models. The study compares different tasks. Its design provides no controlled test in which one task's length, number of dependent steps or amount of agent context is varied while keeping the remaining conditions fixed. [ArXiv v4, §2.3, Fig. 3 and Appendix C.3](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.14499v4) · [NeurIPS paper, pp. 4–5](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2025/file/85069585133c4c168c865e65d72e9775-Paper-Conference.pdf#page=4)

For each agent, the authors fit a logistic curve using the log of rated human duration and binary success, with separate fitted parameters for the midpoint and slope. When the curve crosses 50%, the associated human task duration is the agent's 50% horizon. On this suite o3's 50% horizon was around 110 minutes, and it succeeded on some tasks rated at over four hours. The 80% horizons were reported as roughly four to six times shorter than the 50% horizons. The sparse data around some durations and the discontinuity between SWAA and HCAST make the smooth curve an approximation. Appendix E.3 also explains that estimating horizons at very high success probabilities needs far more tasks and very little scoring noise. A horizon is a benchmark estimate of success on tasks of a given human-rated duration. An agent can fail on shorter tasks and succeed on longer ones, and its own wall-clock time is a different measurement. [ArXiv v4, §§3.1–3.2.1, Figs. 4 and 17, and Appendix E.3](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.14499v4) · [NeurIPS paper, pp. 5–7](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2025/file/85069585133c4c168c865e65d72e9775-Paper-Conference.pdf#page=5)

### Ways agents get stuck and what the study can establish about them

The authors manually labeled failed runs from GPT-4 1106 and o1. Repeated failed actions accounted for 12 of 31 sampled GPT-4 1106 failures and two of 32 sampled o1 failures. Premature abandonment accounted for eight of 31 and 16 of 32, respectively. The two groups contain only failures, and o1's group came from generally harder tasks because o1 completed more of the suite. These are descriptive failure categories and selected denominators. They supply evidence of failures involving repeated tactics and early submission, with differences in prevalence within the samples. The categorization cannot estimate a model-wide per-step error probability or isolate how much better recovery contributed to the horizon difference. [ArXiv v4, §3.3, Table 2 and Appendix D.1](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.14499v4) · [NeurIPS paper, p. 7](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2025/file/85069585133c4c168c865e65d72e9775-Paper-Conference.pdf#page=7)

Appendix D.2 illustrates the difference between an initial error and the agent's response. In a Python debugging example, a Claude 3.5 Sonnet run edited the wrong location, then encountered indentation errors and leftover duplicate methods while trying to repair its edits. It eventually changed approach and rewrote the file successfully. Another run using GPT-4 Turbo introduced a syntax error and gave up after unsuccessful repair attempts. The examples show that local mistakes can create additional work and that agents sometimes recover. They leave open how often an early mistake propagates across dependent stages before causing a failure. The paper's observations about missing checks and failure to seek available information give other plausible explanations for poor completion rates on difficult tasks. [ArXiv v4, Appendix D.2.1–D.2.2](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.14499v4)

### Task conditions and the baseline can change the inference

The authors rated HCAST and RE-Bench tasks on 16 binary properties they call messiness factors. The properties include such conditions as novelty, scarce resources, dynamic environments, and difficulty recovering from some mistakes. The two suites averaged 3.2 out of 16, and none of the rated tasks scored above eight. Figure 13 reports that on HCAST the residual difference between observed success and the prediction from task duration tended to decline as the messiness score increased. The linear approximation was about 8.1 percentage points per score point in that analysis. The comparison remains observational and the estimate does not assign a causal effect to one additional condition. Figure 12 separately shows similar improvement across model release dates in the higher- and lower-messiness subsets within this limited range. [ArXiv v4, §4, Appendix F.2, Figs. 12–13 and Appendix H.6](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.14499v4)

Five real issues from one internal METR repository give the human reference group practical consequences. The same work could be much quicker when done by a maintainer. On one issue, the maintainer and contractor each produced fixes that received the top grade, in five and 81 minutes respectively. The paper summarizes the observed contractor-to-maintainer slowdown as about five- to eighteen-fold, with some comparisons unavailable and some contractor submissions receiving partial grades. On this small exercise, agent results were more consistent with what the main-suite curves predict when calibrated to contractor duration than when calibrated to maintainer duration. The study also ran a separate comparison on SWE-bench Verified using annotated times and reported model scores. Its shorter estimated historical doubling time illustrates how the benchmark and the human timing method can change an estimate. [ArXiv v4, §4, §4.1 and Appendix C.2, Tables 3–5](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.14499v4)

## Extractable practitioner insights

### Calibrate confidence to the work the agent must complete

For OB-08, a team that sees good results on isolated edits and uneven results on multi-part changes can examine where the larger change adds dependencies and what counts as complete. Say an agent can update one export formatter, while adding a format across the command-line interface, background job and user documentation requires state to agree across those surfaces. The team can distinguish amount of work from uncertainty about decisions and from how easily it can verify the integration. This is a catalog application of duration as a risk signal and of the evidence that reference groups and task conditions matter. Estimating a local pass rate requires representative local tasks and explicit completion criteria. Claims SRC-0016:C03–C05 and C08–C10. [ArXiv v4, §§2.3–4, Fig. 4 and Appendix E.3](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.14499v4)

### Look at how the failure develops

When several dependent edits fail, a useful catalog adaptation is to identify the first unsupported assumption or failed check and see which later work relies on it. For example, a new parser may return a value that differs from the data model expected by the next stage. A focused boundary check helps the maintainer see whether later failures arise from that disagreement or require their own explanation. A candidate checkpoint or boundary check is a workflow choice. The paper evaluated complete autonomous attempts and did not compare the same end deliverable with and without decomposition or reviewer checkpoints. For a distinct practitioner description of vertical slicing, see the AI Hero dossier. Claims SRC-0016:C06–C08. [ArXiv v4, Appendix D](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.14499v4) · [SRC-0006, The /to-tickets Skill](SRC-0006--vertical-slices-for-agent-tickets.md)

## Related work, conflicts, and limitations

- **Reused benchmark data.** The paper explicitly reuses HCAST tasks and baselines and RE-Bench environments. Those original benchmark publications provide provenance for major components of the evidence. METR's *Time Horizon 1.1* documents substantial overlap in task and model sets. Its updated curves are a follow-on measurement under changed sampling and infrastructure. The NeurIPS report, arXiv v4 and METR's launch write-up are accounts of the same research project. [ArXiv v4, §§1.1, 2.1 and Appendix C.1](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.14499v4) · [METR launch write-up](https://evals.alignment.org/blog/2025-03-19-measuring-ai-ability-to-complete-long-tasks/) · [METR Time Horizon 1.1, measurement comparison and appendix](https://metr.org/blog/2026-01-29-time-horizon-1-1/)
- **Separate analysis within the same evaluation program.** METR's reward-hacking report analyzes agent runs on HCAST and RE-Bench and shares authors with this paper. It documents consequences of benchmark scoring defects and says that METR attempts to exclude detected successes obtained through such shortcuts from capability evaluations. The exact overlap of individual runs with the time-horizon analysis was not established. Shared datasets and investigators limit its use as independent evidence of benchmark reliability. [ArXiv v4, §2.1 and Appendix B.1.3, n. 13](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.14499v4) · [METR reward-hacking report, “What we've observed” and nn. 4–5](https://metr.org/blog/2025-06-05-recent-reward-hacking/) · [SRC-0014, benchmark shortcuts and validation](SRC-0014--benchmark-shortcuts-and-validation.md)
- **Different experiment with shared authors.** Becker et al.'s METR trial randomized AI access for experienced contributors doing their own real open-source issues and measured the time taken by the developers. It uses a different task sample, treatment and outcome from autonomous success on this benchmark. It bears on the importance of repository familiarity and real verification work. The four Becker et al. authors also appear on the v4 paper. These are distinct datasets from the same institution and address different questions. [Becker et al., §§1–2.3](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.09089v2) · [Kwa et al., v4 author list](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.14499v4)
- **Causal gap for OB-08.** Human duration combines several sources of effort, and the benchmark has no controlled contrast that adds dependencies while holding task content and evaluation fixed. A sequence of dependent operations can propagate an early error, but steps may also overlap, share a common cause, or include checks that repair the mistake. The source gives no empirical basis for multiplying a fixed per-step success rate to predict project success. It also leaves open the benefits and costs of splitting the same deliverable. [ArXiv v4, §§2.1–3.3 and Appendices C.1.5, D and E.3](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.14499v4)

## Retrieval hints

- “The coding agent succeeds on small fixes but loses track once a change crosses a few components.”
- “Does a two-hour time horizon mean an agent can work for two hours by itself?”
- “Is a task hard for the agent because it takes me longer, because our human baselines differ, or because the intermediate results are hard to check?”
- “Does the time-horizon paper show that early mistakes compound or that slicing the same task improves success?”
- Search terms include autonomous software agents, task completion probability, human reference duration, task dependencies, 50% time horizon, error recovery and repository familiarity.
