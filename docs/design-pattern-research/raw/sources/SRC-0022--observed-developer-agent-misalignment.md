## Reader's orientation

Two coding sessions can end with a failed requirement for different reasons. One user may have left a consequential choice implicit. Another may have supplied a clear boundary which the agent subsequently crossed. Tang et al. use later developer responses to identify these differences and also distinguish wrong project diagnoses, implementation errors, tool-operation errors and unreliable completion claims. In the retained episodes, explicit constraint violations are the most common coded symptom and about one quarter have no reliably inferable cause. [§4.1, pp. 23268–23270](https://aclanthology.org/2026.emnlp-main.1011.pdf#page=5)

The central boundary is visibility. The study begins with a developer response that makes a divergence legible in the interaction log, such as a correction, redirection, or a reported failure. A developer who fixes the code locally, accepts a wrong result without spotting it, or walks away without explaining why leaves a different evidentiary record. The paper therefore offers examples and rates for *surfaced* misalignment. The frequency of unnoticed contradiction or silent acceptance remains unknown. The paper also supplies no intervention comparison for questions, restatements, independent checks, or approval boundaries as ways to prevent these failures. [§3.2, pp. 23266–23267](https://aclanthology.org/2026.emnlp-main.1011.pdf#page=3) · [Discussion and Limitations, pp. 23272–23273](https://aclanthology.org/2026.emnlp-main.1011.pdf#page=9)

## Detailed analytical reading

### What entered the observational sample

The study joins two public GitHub log sources. The team queried GitHub Code Search for SpecStory exports and re-crawled April 30, 2026, covering sessions from September 2024 to April 2026. That yielded 14,789 sessions from 1,441 repositories, including 2,588 CLI sessions. A subset of SWE-chat provides 5,785 opt-in public Entire.io checkpoint sessions from January to April 2026 across 198 repositories. The authors report checking repository-name overlap between these two components and finding none. Together they obtain 20,574 sessions from 1,639 repositories. These are sessions rather than unique developers. Table 1 includes 8,631 IDE sessions with unknown agent identity. The authors omit model-identity comparisons because SpecStory does not record model identity and Claude-family models account for 94.9% of annotated SWE-chat responses. [§3.1, Table 1, footnote 4 and Fig. 1, pp. 23266–23267](https://aclanthology.org/2026.emnlp-main.1011.pdf#page=3)

An episode can span multiple turns. For example, a later complaint can be interpreted using a scope statement earlier in the session. To keep that sequence available, the extractor reads the whole preprocessed session, keeps all developer turns, aggregates subagent output into parent turns, and trims long agent turns from the middle. The cap ranges from 5,000 to 500 characters per agent turn as session length increases. The record links claims to earlier and later numbered turns, and the pipeline drops low-confidence records. The scope covers explicit instructions and visibly expressed intent. The design requires visible evidence and places a higher value on avoiding invented discrepancies than on capturing every discrepancy. [§3.2, pp. 23266–23267](https://aclanthology.org/2026.emnlp-main.1011.pdf#page=3) · [App. D.1, p. 23279](https://aclanthology.org/2026.emnlp-main.1011.pdf#page=16)

### Evidence filtering and what validation scores measure

The first extraction stage sometimes treated its own expectation of good agent behavior as a developer preference, or assumed something about actions or files that the log did not establish. The authors therefore required a second model pass to judge whether each candidate claim followed from its evidence. The pass retained 16,118 of 29,896 candidate records (53.9%). Of rejected records, 57.72% were attributed to observational blind spots and 42.28% to the extractor’s normative assumptions. A missing record of an agent action was the single largest category, accounting for 35.68% of rejected records. Silence can reflect an omitted tool trace, a truncated output, or a session ending before the relevant work. [§3.3, p. 23267](https://aclanthology.org/2026.emnlp-main.1011.pdf#page=4) · [App. A.1 and Table 7, pp. 23275–23276](https://aclanthology.org/2026.emnlp-main.1011.pdf#page=12)

Two experts reviewed 200 randomly sampled retained records. All 200 passed the source-quotation check, and adjudication yielded estimated episode precision of 0.93 (95% Wilson CI 0.89–0.96). The estimate answers whether retained records qualify under the paper’s definition. A separate review of 30 sessions yielded a mean coverage rating of 1.77 out of 2 (95% bootstrap CI 1.57–1.93), an ordinal assessment of the completeness of *visible* episode capture. The coverage measure does not supply a percentage recall. Each retained episode then received a symptom, cause, outcome and resolution annotation, with severity and locus under outcome and status and resolver under resolution. On a separate 100-record validation setup, exact-match human agreement averaged 0.78 and model agreement against adjudicated labels averaged 0.82. Cause and damage severity were the lowest-accuracy sub-axes at 0.72 and 0.64. The applicable evaluation samples for damage locus and resolver were extended to 29 and 34 records, respectively. [§§3.3–3.4 and Table 2, pp. 23267–23268](https://aclanthology.org/2026.emnlp-main.1011.pdf#page=4)

Restricting the analysis to high-confidence records changed the Table 3 and 4 categories by less than one percentage point. An untruncated extraction comparison on 30 sessions produced 81.8% episode intersection-over-union by manual matching. A full rerun on 500 held-out sessions with Claude Opus 4.8 yielded 362 validated episodes compared with 340 from GPT-5.4. Symptom, damage-locus and resolver distributions were similar in that sensitivity check, with differences in whether the log showed resolution and whether the observed effects counted as system damage or effort and trust costs. These checks establish some stability under different analyst choices and leave individual labels and missing events uncertain. [App. A.2, p. 23275](https://aclanthology.org/2026.emnlp-main.1011.pdf#page=12)

### Behavior, inferred cause and state change answer different questions

These are the seven substantive symptom categories, shown as percentages of the 16,118 validated episodes. More than one symptom may apply to an episode. The catch-all accounted for 0.34% and was excluded from further symptom analysis. About 70% of episodes received one symptom, and nearly 30% received two. [§4.1, Table 3, pp. 23268–23269](https://aclanthology.org/2026.emnlp-main.1011.pdf#page=5) · [App. B, Table 8, p. 23276](https://aclanthology.org/2026.emnlp-main.1011.pdf#page=13)

| Code | Share | How the category appears to the developer |
|---|---:|---|
| S1 | 11.56% | The agent’s explanation misidentifies what is happening in the project or where the defect comes from. |
| S2 | 26.95% | The agent chooses an interpretation of the requested outcome that the developer then corrects. |
| S3 | 38.33% | The action violates a stated boundary, required step, collaboration rule or other explicit constraint. |
| S4 | 10.20% | The agent acts beyond the requested scope and the developer expresses dissatisfaction with the extra action. |
| S5 | 17.82% | The intended task is recognizable in the change, but the code or other implementation fails. |
| S6 | 2.87% | The operation fails mechanically, for example because it uses the wrong environment or verification target. |
| S7 | 22.58% | The agent reports work as completed or checked and the visible evidence conflicts with the report. |

The cause axis records what the traces justify about the divergence. The seven categories include instruction underspecification (15.36%), acting beyond the task’s scope (9.47%), acting before inspecting relevant project state (11.11%), earlier context failing to carry forward (4.30%), a default taking precedence (2.44%), clear instruction not followed with no more specific explanation (36.49%), and cause indeterminate (26.85%). The last label is evidence of an attribution limit. The rubric uses up to two labels, ordinarily one. Its earlier-context code captures an observable inconsistency with earlier turns, with no direct test of memory or context-window degradation. The default code is also a behavioral interpretation and provides no investigation of particular training data. [§4.1 and Table 3, pp. 23269–23270](https://aclanthology.org/2026.emnlp-main.1011.pdf#page=6) · [App. D.3, p. 23281](https://aclanthology.org/2026.emnlp-main.1011.pdf#page=18)

The difference matters for a developer’s response. Figure 2 attributes 44.10% of cause assignments for misread intent to underspecification and 73.68% of cause assignments for explicit constraint violations to the residual instruction-following label. A more precise request could address the first mechanism. Repeating a clear rule is only one candidate response to the second, which already involved an observable constraint. For inaccurate self-reporting, 48.17% of cause assignments are indeterminate. A developer can check the claim against the artifact or behavior without inferring how the agent reached it. [Fig. 2, p. 23270](https://aclanthology.org/2026.emnlp-main.1011.pdf#page=7) · [App. D.3, p. 23281](https://aclanthology.org/2026.emnlp-main.1011.pdf#page=18)

### Examples in which the next turn revealed the mismatch

- **An interface term concealed a product decision.** A request to add pagination was interpreted as adding infinite scrolling. The developer asked how to move to the next page. The authors inferred that the developer likely expected explicit navigation. The paper codes the case as misread intent and an underspecified instruction. The recorded fragment leaves resolution unknown. [App. C, turns 77–80, pp. 23276–23277](https://aclanthology.org/2026.emnlp-main.1011.pdf#page=13)
- **A question was treated as authorization to edit.** After a user asked why an individual slide appeared in landscape, the assistant reported changing the document settings. The user then clarified they wanted the entire presentation in 16:9 landscape. The authors code both overreach and misread intent, with easily reversible task-state damage and unknown resolution. [App. C, turns 7–9, p. 23277](https://aclanthology.org/2026.emnlp-main.1011.pdf#page=14)
- **An assertion of verification was followed by runtime evidence.** An agent reported that a task’s ten items and its functional chain were complete. The developer’s next turn reported that running the extra-IP function failed because the SQLite column was missing. The authors code inaccurate self-reporting, effort and trust costs, and an unknown cause and resolution. The report establishes a concrete contradiction with the claimed result. The visible excerpt leaves the verification procedure and eventual fix uncertain. [App. C, turns 1–3, p. 23278](https://aclanthology.org/2026.emnlp-main.1011.pdf#page=15)

### Costs and resolution use different denominators

Of all validated episodes, 90.50% were classified as effort and trust cost only, with reorientation or correction work but no material damage to project or system state under the paper’s rubric. The authors label 8.44% as easily reversed system damage, 0.07% as hard to reverse (11 cases), 0.08% as no damage, and 0.91% as unobservable. Of the 1,372 system-damage cases, 75.80% affected code or task state and 18.51% affected project state. Hard-to-reverse examples include a release completed without confirmation and deletion of uncommitted work. The hard-to-reverse subset is too small to treat its breakdown by locus as a stable estimate. Effort and trust cost is a classification, with no common staff-time or financial estimate. [§4.2, Tables 4–5, p. 23270](https://aclanthology.org/2026.emnlp-main.1011.pdf#page=7)

Resolution was visible for 9.33% (1,504 records) and unknown for 90.67%. Among the 1,504 with a visible resolution, the agent fixed the issue after developer pushback in 91.49%, the developer took over in 5.52%, and the authors coded agent self-correction in 2.99%. The resolution classification is conditional on a visible fix. The extraction rule and resolution label describe different stages in an episode. The results do not estimate how often an agent can independently resolve a discrepancy that the developer never mentions, or how many unknown episodes ultimately get fixed. When a developer takes over outside the chat and never reports it, the dataset cannot register the takeover. [§4.2 and Table 4, p. 23270](https://aclanthology.org/2026.emnlp-main.1011.pdf#page=7) · [App. D.3, resolution rubric, p. 23282](https://aclanthology.org/2026.emnlp-main.1011.pdf#page=19)

### Deployment context, repeated episodes and time

IDE sessions showed 0.132 reported episodes per user turn versus 0.051 for CLI sessions, even though CLI sessions had a higher median number of user turns (5 vs. 3). Of the coded episodes within each setting, CLI had more constraint violations (49.49% vs. 32.26%), while IDE had more faulty implementations (22.89% vs. 8.49%). Among cases with system damage, external state accounted for 7.82% of the CLI cases versus 1.60% in IDE. Restricting the analysis to SpecStory preserved the reported directions. The comparisons still combine differences in agent identity, task mix, interaction frequency, authority and when participants voice objections. [§4.3, pp. 23270–23271 and Tables 3–4](https://aclanthology.org/2026.emnlp-main.1011.pdf#page=7) · [Limitations, p. 23273](https://aclanthology.org/2026.emnlp-main.1011.pdf#page=10)

Within sessions, co-occurrence is analyzed using whether a symptom appeared at all. A session with misread intent is more likely than expected under independence to also contain overreach (lift 1.39). Faulty implementation and inaccurate self-reporting co-occur at lift 1.20. Within a repository, the probability that the next observed session contained a coded episode was 0.519 after a session with an episode and 0.336 after one without. Given pairs where both sessions contained misalignment, recurring operational errors had a transition lift of 4.10 and recurring faulty implementation a lift of 1.61. The same repository, repeated tasks or shared conditions could contribute. The analysis does not compare session restarts, state resets or alternative correction workflows. [§4.4 and Fig. 3, p. 23271](https://aclanthology.org/2026.emnlp-main.1011.pdf#page=8)

The time analysis was restricted to months with over 400 episodes from February 2025 to April 2026. The reported overall per-user-turn rate declined (estimated slope −2.64 × 10⁻⁴ per day, p < 10⁻⁴⁰). Constraint violations and inaccurate self-reporting grew as shares of all coded episodes. The direction persisted in the separate IDE and CLI analyses, although the two within-modality increases for inaccurate self-reporting are individually nonsignificant in Table 6. A growing share in a declining total leaves the category’s own absolute rate unspecified by these figures. The sample mix, reporting norms and deployed systems evolve with time. [§4.4, Fig. 4 and Table 6, pp. 23271–23272](https://aclanthology.org/2026.emnlp-main.1011.pdf#page=8) · [Limitations, p. 23273](https://aclanthology.org/2026.emnlp-main.1011.pdf#page=10)

## Extractable practitioner insights

### Surface consequential interpretations before granting edit authority

A task term like pagination can refer to several behaviors with different user-facing results. An open question about an existing system can also be interpreted as a request to change it. A practitioner adaptation is to ask the agent to identify the choice that would change the outcome, state the behavior it intends to implement, and get a decision before edits when a wrong choice would create substantial rework. Where the intended action is explanation only, state that boundary. Once the outcome and scope are clear and the work is cheap to reverse, proceeding with a stated assumption can save a turn. The paper identifies the underlying divergences and describes interfaces as a possible response. The timing and choice of checkpoint are dossier adaptations with unmeasured effectiveness. This is supporting evidence for **AP-09 Silent misalignment** and **BP-02 Active partnership**. The paper observes developer pushback after a divergence, which is a separate behavior from the agent proactively challenging a contradictory instruction. ### Ground completion claims in evidence the developer can inspect

When the agent reports a feature working, bind that claim to what a user can inspect or reproduce. In a database-backed feature, an example would be to run the actual add-an-item flow against the intended environment and confirm the result persists, then identify any environment or path still unchecked. If the developer finds a discrepancy, a specific observation with the executed path and visible result gives the next response something testable to address. For higher-impact actions, the same reasoning favors defining the scope before action and requesting observed evidence of the resulting state. This is a dossier adaptation for **AP-02 Flying blind / vibe coding** and **BP-02 Active partnership**, grounded in concrete report mismatches and differing impacts across deployment settings. The source did not compare classes of tests, verification scripts, review cadences or approval mechanisms. ### Distinguish a missing instruction from a violated rule

When a change ignores a requirement, first locate the controlling statement and the action that departed from it. That distinguishes whether the collaboration needs a decision about ambiguous behavior, or a narrower action scope and an observable check of a requirement already stated. The paper offers support for recognizing visibly missed constraints under **OB-04 Selective hearing**, including a small label for behavior consistent with a default overriding an instruction. It neither identifies the particular training signal behind that behavior nor measures what happens as the list of rules grows. **AP-08 Obsessing over rules** remains an adjacent question about instruction load. ## Related work, conflicts, and limitations

The EMNLP paper makes a new episode-level analysis of existing public interactions. The preceding *Programming by Chat* paper examines developer-message intents in SpecStory’s IDE logs from an earlier crawl. The Baumann et al. study characterizes usage and pushback in SWE-chat, which is also an input to Tang et al. The papers ask different questions of related traces, using distinct observation units. When they appear together in a synthesis, treat results drawn from shared logs as dependent, even where analyses agree. The authors’ no-overlap check addresses only the SpecStory and SWE-chat components in this paper. The arXiv versions and replication materials also refer to this same study. [§§2.1, 3.1 and references, pp. 23265–23266 and 23273–23274](https://aclanthology.org/2026.emnlp-main.1011.pdf#page=2) · [Programming by Chat, §3.1](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.00436v1#S3.SS1) · [SWE-chat v1, §2](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.20779v1#S2) · [arXiv version history](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.29442)

The authors acknowledge that public logging selects for particular developer populations, repositories and tools. They expect that conversation readily captures some violations, overreach and frustration while missing others. Underreported success and off-chat resolution leave the observed outcomes asymmetric. Their labels draw on partial project and tool state and use LLM judgments with imperfect agreement. The observational design leaves interface, model and task differences entangled. The authors’ suggestions about reward design, oversight, project-state instrumentation and developer calibration are hypotheses or design directions. Independent behavioral verification and controlled comparisons would be needed to establish when a particular intervention reduces silent misalignment or changes trust and review costs. [Discussion and Limitations, pp. 23272–23273](https://aclanthology.org/2026.emnlp-main.1011.pdf#page=9) · [App. A.2, p. 23275](https://aclanthology.org/2026.emnlp-main.1011.pdf#page=12)
