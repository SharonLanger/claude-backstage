## Reader's orientation

The paper asks how often a further model repair salvages a still-failing generated solution and whether spending a later attempt on a new solution improves the final solve rate. In the main experiment, the jobs are short Python functions and the feedback comes from executing tests. Many models show diminishing rescue rates over successive repairs. On the nine models listed in the restart comparison, at least one of the two restart timings raises final solve rate relative to continuous debugging. Two models do worse at the earlier threshold. [Publisher PDF, pp. 4–8](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-27846-5.pdf?code=7b2&error=cookies_not_supported) · [Table 2](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-27846-5/tables/2?code=7b2&error=cookies_not_supported)

This is evidence about a test-driven retry policy. Ordinary repairs depend on earlier attempts. A fresh start clears the failed attempt history and asks the model to solve the original problem again. That design leaves the cause of the baseline decline open, including whether an accumulating conversation made the model worse at a fixed task. [Publisher PDF, pp. 4–5, 9](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-27846-5.pdf?code=7b2&error=cookies_not_supported)

## Detailed analytical reading

### How the benchmark simulates a debugging session

The initial comparison covers 18 model configurations and HumanEval's 164 function-level Python problems. Attempt 0 generates code from the problem specification. Failed code proceeds to one or more correction attempts. The next generation receives the original specification, previous generated code, execution feedback such as a trace or failed test output, and a request to fix the code. The revised solution runs against the same test cases. A problem leaves the retry pool at its first success or reaches the cap of five repairs following the first generation. This operationalizes a sequential feedback loop in which each correction depends on a generated predecessor. [Publisher PDF, pp. 4–5](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-27846-5.pdf?code=7b2&error=cookies_not_supported)

The DDI introduction frames the baseline as continued refinement in the same generation context, its methods specify usable predecessor information, and its discussion proposes accumulation of revisions and errors as a mechanism. The cited antecedent PyCapsule v2 describes its normal repair policy in §3.1 as retaining only the most recent problem, solution and error pair. Its §F.2 fix-mode prompt refers to that input as conversation history. The DDI methods give an abbreviated description of the borrowed protocol and leave the exact history retention policy, serialized prompts and provider session handling for its runs unverified. Carrying the immediately preceding state across attempts creates dependence even if the system truncates older turns. Full dialogue accumulation would require documented exposure or run-level evidence. [Publisher PDF, pp. 2, 4–5, 9–10](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-27846-5.pdf?code=7b2&error=cookies_not_supported) · [PyCapsule v2, §§3.1 and F.2](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.02928v2)

Three kinds of retry are useful to distinguish for citation. Continuous repair conditions each generation on the failure chain and test feedback. A strategic fresh start allocates a slot in that chain to a fresh generation from the original specification, after earlier generations have already had a chance to solve the problem. A budget spent entirely on fresh generations would instead compare independently generated candidates, as in the paper's description of `pass@k`. The reported restart comparison has the first two policies as its arms. It gives no solve-rate estimate for a full fresh-draw budget. [Publisher PDF, p. 2, pp. 4–5, pp. 6–8](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-27846-5.pdf?code=7b2&error=cookies_not_supported)

### What decays and what can improve simultaneously

For attempt `i`, let `S_i` count problems first solved by that attempt and `N_i` count those still unsolved immediately before it. The measured rescue rate is `I_i = S_i/N_i`. At the first generation, the denominator is all tasks and `I_0 = E_0`, the initial test-pass proportion. Later denominators shrink and their task composition changes. The paper calls each repair's contribution independent in the sense that previous successes are counted separately. The observations still come from a sequence of dependent attempts. `A_0` in Table 2 is the cumulative final accuracy for the no-restart policy. It includes problems solved on any attempt and can rise even while an additional attempt's rescue rate falls. [Publisher PDF, p. 5, pp. 6–7](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-27846-5.pdf?code=7b2&error=cookies_not_supported)

One possible statistical contributor is the changing pool of problems. For illustration, suppose half the tasks have a fixed 90% success chance per attempt and half have a fixed 10%, with independent attempts within each task. The expected first-pass rate is 50%. Among the survivors of the first attempt, about 10% are from the easier group and 90% from the harder group. The group's next rescue rate is then 18% even though each individual task's success probability is unchanged. This is an analyst illustration, not an alternative fit to the paper's raw results. It shows why normalization for the number of unresolved tasks alone cannot identify how much of the observed decline came from any change within a task's repair history. Initial generation and repair also use different information and instructions. [Underlying measure, publisher PDF, p. 5](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-27846-5.pdf?code=7b2&error=cookies_not_supported)

The proposed Debugging Decay Index (DDI) fits `E(t) = E_0 exp(-λt)` to normalized effectiveness using nonlinear least squares and reports `(E_0, λ, t_θ, R²)`. Its scheduling rule is `ceil(ln(100/(100−θ))/λ)` for a modeled relative loss of `θ` percent from the initial effectiveness. For example, `θ = 80` leaves 20% of the modeled starting effectiveness. It describes a fitted relative change, with no implication that 80% of tasks fail or that a developer should restart when a conversation reaches a particular token count. The paper omits a fit when fewer than three usable observations remain. Table 1 explains that zero-effectiveness points are filtered. The paper marks `R² ≥ 0.9` excellent, `0.7 ≤ R² < 0.9` good and `R² < 0.7` poor. Thresholds beyond the observed five-repair budget are extrapolations. [Publisher PDF, pp. 3–6](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-27846-5.pdf?code=7b2&error=cookies_not_supported) · [Supplement, Algorithm 1, p. 1](https://static-content.springer.com/esm/art:10.1038%2Fs41598-025-27846-5/MediaObjects/41598_2025_27846_MOESM1_ESM.pdf)

Table 1 records 13 excellent fits, one good fit, three poor fits and one absent fit. For example, GPT-3.5-turbo has `λ = 1.3297` and `t_80 = 2`, while Codestral:22b has `λ = 0.3388` and `t_80 = 5`. GPT-4-1106-preview has high final accuracy (96.951%) and a poor fit. Claude-3.7-Sonnet begins at 93.902%, reaches 100% by about two attempts according to the authors, and has too few informative points for a fit. The Phi-4 example also separates overall success from improvement across repairs. Phi-4-reasoning:14b rises from 59.146% initially to 81.098% finally, a 21.952 percentage-point gain. Phi-4:14b rises from 83.537% to 93.293%, a smaller 9.756-point gain with higher final accuracy. These examples make the abstract's characterization of fast relative drops a benchmark-level description, with exceptions in both the fit and the timescale. The numerical decay estimates here use Table 1 where prose in the Evaluation section gives different figures. [Table 1](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-27846-5/tables/1?code=7b2&error=cookies_not_supported) · [Publisher PDF, pp. 5–7](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-27846-5.pdf?code=7b2&error=cookies_not_supported)

### What the strategic fresh start changes

For still-unsolved problems, the intervention removes the prior conversation and starts the generation again with the original problem statement. The baseline continues revising the failed solution with the ordinary feedback loop. Each arm has the same maximum of six total attempts per problem, and the restart uses the existing budget. The two intervention arms schedule their reset according to model-specific `t_50` and `t_80` values estimated by the decay model. This simultaneously removes the old messages and replaces the task of repairing the existing candidate with generation from its specification. The publication compares those two schedules with continued repair. Comparisons with random restart timing, a pure fresh-sampling policy, a different approach generated with the history intact, or a shorter prompt that preserves the failed candidate are outside the reported design. The publication also does not provide a detailed restart trace for determining the precise off-by-one convention of an implementation or its handling of repeated resets. [Publisher PDF, pp. 4–5, pp. 6–8](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-27846-5.pdf?code=7b2&error=cookies_not_supported) · [Supplement, Algorithm 1, p. 1](https://static-content.springer.com/esm/art:10.1038%2Fs41598-025-27846-5/MediaObjects/41598_2025_27846_MOESM1_ESM.pdf)

In publisher Table 2, all nine listed models improve at the 80%-loss schedule. Seven of those nine also improve at the 50%-loss schedule. At `t_50`, Llama 3.1 8B goes from 72.5610% to 82.3171% (+9.7561 percentage points), DeepSeek-Coder-V2 16B from 84.1463% to 92.0732% (+7.9269 points), and Mistral:Instruct from 54.2683% to 62.8049% (+8.5366 points). Mistral's later `t_80` restart yields a smaller rise, to 57.3171%, while Codestral:22b goes from 89.0244% to 88.4146% at `t_50` and 91.4634% at `t_80`. Devstral:24b also loses 0.6098 points at `t_50` and gains 0.6098 points at `t_80`. The accompanying prose says six models and gives an 82.82% Llama result. The publisher PDF and HTML table agree with each other on nine rows and 82.3171%. The prose also calls DeepSeek's gain second largest, while the displayed Mistral gain at `t_50` is larger. The dossier uses the table cells and avoids relying on the prose's ranking. [Table 2](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-27846-5/tables/2?code=7b2&error=cookies_not_supported) · [Publisher PDF, pp. 6–7](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-27846-5.pdf?code=7b2&error=cookies_not_supported)

Figure 2 illustrates that restart runs can regain rescue rate at intervention points. The authors report a maximum attempt budget shared by arms and token examples in which fresh starts use fewer tokens. DeepSeek-Coder-V2 goes from 108,289 to roughly 89,000 tokens on average, and Codestral goes from about 97,000 to about 94,000. The intervention table gives point estimates without uncertainty intervals or significance tests for its accuracy differences. The authors' equal-overhead language is strongest as a statement about the attempt cap and the reported token examples. Actual wall-clock time and costs for a practitioner will depend on prompt size, the task, the model and the effort required to prepare and evaluate a second implementation. [Publisher PDF, pp. 6–8](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-27846-5.pdf?code=7b2&error=cookies_not_supported)

### Cross-dataset scope and the authors' explanations

Three models were run on HumanEval, HumanEval-ET, MBPP and MBPP-ET to compare fitted rates and thresholds. Table 3 reports model-specific differences and some stability across these related programming benchmarks. For GPT-4-1106-preview, fitted rates range from 0.573 to 0.743 and continuous `t_80` estimates from 2.17 to 2.81 on these datasets. The authors report a one-way ANOVA of the fitted rates, `F(2, 9) = 10.04`, `p < 0.01`, `η² = 0.691`. This is an analysis of the three model groups' derived decay rates. Restart gains are a different outcome and are reported in the HumanEval comparison. Four related coding benchmarks and three models offer limited grounds for treating decay as primarily a model trait across different software work. The authors also report differences between the HumanEval fits in Tables 1 and 3 and attribute them to sampling variation. [Table 3](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-27846-5/tables/3?code=7b2&error=cookies_not_supported) · [Publisher PDF, pp. 7–9](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-27846-5.pdf?code=7b2&error=cookies_not_supported)

The authors suggest three mechanisms in Discussion. A larger history may make initial requirements harder to attend to. Revisions may compound errors or drift from instructions. Repairs may repeatedly vary an approach that cannot meet the specification, and a fresh generation may explore a different one. The experiment observes test outcomes across retry policies, with no separate measures of instruction retention, error compounding, or similarity of successive approaches, so these are explanations to test. The authors leave adaptive threshold choice and testing across different coding domains to future work. [Publisher PDF, pp. 9–10](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-27846-5.pdf?code=7b2&error=cookies_not_supported)

## Extractable practitioner insights

### Treat a fresh implementation as an option when repeated repairs stop making progress

**Source evidence.** A bounded number of test-driven repairs still helps many models, and switching some attempts to a clean generation improves the final solve fraction for the tested models at one or both schedules. The authors schedule the intervention with model-level aggregate data. They leave selection of an optimal threshold open. `SRC-0003:C02`, `C05`–`C09`, `C11`. [Publisher PDF, pp. 5–9](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-27846-5.pdf?code=7b2&error=cookies_not_supported)

**Practitioner adaptation.** For a small defect with an executable acceptance check, give a bounded repair attempt the failing output and inspect whether the evidence changes. If successive patches replay the same hypothesis and leave the same checks failing, keep a recoverable copy and try a fresh implementation from a verified task statement in an isolated context. Carry requirements, constraints and the test to run into that context. Run both candidates against the same checks before choosing. For example, when successive fixes to a date parser preserve the same off-by-one error around month boundaries, a fresh implementation can begin from the accepted boundary rules and tests, without the provisional parsing algorithm. Tests that are inadequate and changes that are expensive to recreate make this adaptation less diagnostic. The qualitative trigger, recoverable copy, external acceptance checks and any additional review are catalog adaptations. Study topics are AP-01 and BP-03, with a bounded application to BP-06.

### Use repeated ties to the same failed approach as a diagnostic prompt

**Source interpretation.** The authors propose that retries can remain within variations of a failing approach. The results of replacing a repair with a clean generation are compatible with that idea. The paper leaves the causal mechanism open. `SRC-0003:C06`–`C07`, `C10`. [Publisher PDF, pp. 7, 9](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-27846-5.pdf?code=7b2&error=cookies_not_supported)

**Practitioner adaptation.** Before spending another repair attempt, ask which implementation assumption changed and which test would fail if the assumption were wrong. If the proposed patch holds every relevant assumption fixed, compare a genuinely different approach on the same acceptance test. This is a way to make the workflow symptom testable under OB-07. For OB-01, use the source as coding-session context for a hypothesis that requires separate direct evidence of instruction or attention degradation. OB-06 has limited supporting relevance via the authors' note on sampling variation. None of these links establishes an independent human-workflow or context-window result.

## Related work, conflicts, and limitations

- The cited PyCapsule paper builds the repair substrate and the attempt-level normalization that DDI reuses. It shares Adnan and Kuhn as authors and overlapping benchmark/model setups. In §3.1, PyCapsule separately reports lower MBPP performance when the Qwen2.5-Coder-7B-Instruct repair prompt retains two or three recent history pairs instead of one. That small ablation belongs to the antecedent work. It cannot resolve the history configuration used for the DDI runs or independently confirm their restart result. The arXiv DDI preprint and journal article are versions of the same work. [PyCapsule v2, §§3–4 and Appendix F](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.02928v2) · [DDI preprint](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.18403)
- The benchmark favors diagnosis when the desired function is expressible and a deterministic test can give a quick correction signal. Repository interactions, systems behavior, incomplete requirements, review effort and long-running human chats introduce outcomes beyond this study's pass/fail measure. Codestral and DeepSeek Coder numerical ranges in the Evaluation narrative differ from Table 1. For both model fits and restart accuracy, use the table and preserve the inconsistencies when reporting source fidelity. [Publisher PDF, pp. 5–9](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-27846-5.pdf?code=7b2&error=cookies_not_supported)
- Data Availability identifies public benchmark repositories and says code and analysis scripts would be released upon publication. The section provides no direct link to run records or implementation scripts. The original per-problem traces and run configuration were unavailable for verification in this reading. The supplement gives the fit procedure and threshold calculation. [Publisher PDF, p. 10, Data availability](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-27846-5.pdf?code=7b2&error=cookies_not_supported) · [Supplement, p. 1](https://static-content.springer.com/esm/art:10.1038%2Fs41598-025-27846-5/MediaObjects/41598_2025_27846_MOESM1_ESM.pdf)
