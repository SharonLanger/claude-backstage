# AI promised to free up workers’ time. UC Berkeley Haas researchers found the opposite.

## Reader's orientation

The source helps explain how workers can experience a tool as helpful on individual tasks and still feel more stretched across the day. Starting more work can change its scope, fill former breaks, and leave people managing more active threads. Ye describes all three forms at one technology company. For AP-04, the most relevant observations are the expansion of work people attempted and the overlap of human work with background AI processes. These are qualitative accounts from one workplace. The interview gives no estimate of how many starts are excessive, how much effort increased, or how often parallel work led to mistakes. [Interview, answers on observed practice and participant experience](https://newsroom.haas.berkeley.edu/ai-promised-to-free-up-workers-time-uc-berkeley-haas-researchers-found-the-opposite/)

## Detailed analytical reading (original words)

### How the investigators noticed changes in work

Ye describes beginning with an exploratory question about how generative AI fits into work. Effort became salient during fieldwork. In eight months at a company whose employees had broad access to generative AI tools, she made regular site visits, watched people organize and switch among tasks, and attended meetings and everyday conversations. She also conducted more than 40 semi-structured interviews across functions. Workflow accounts included tasks employees became willing to undertake, perceived changes in time allocation, and how work felt by day’s end. Real-time observation offers evidence about work practices. Retrospective accounts give access to how workers interpreted those practices and their felt effects. The article’s 200 is company size and the interview count is a count of interviews. The Q&A gives no count of unique interviewees or complete roster of observed workers. [Haas News, lead and first two answers](https://newsroom.haas.berkeley.edu/ai-promised-to-free-up-workers-time-uc-berkeley-haas-researchers-found-the-opposite/)

### What expanded and where it fit into the day

The account describes changes in scope, timing, and simultaneous activity. Scope widened when employees attempted tasks that formerly belonged to another role or might otherwise have stayed undone. Timing changed when a small interaction with the tool fit into an interval previously used as a pause. Ye gives examples around lunch, before scheduled meetings, and during the evening. Simultaneous activity increased when an AI process ran while a worker was already writing, reviewing code, or participating in a meeting. Ye says some staff ran several agents. Background execution can overlap ordinary work even if the human only attends to one task at a time. The interview’s examples identify the overlap. They leave the frequency and cost of switching between results unquantified. [Haas News, third answer](https://newsroom.haas.berkeley.edu/ai-promised-to-free-up-workers-time-uc-berkeley-haas-researchers-found-the-opposite/)

A coding-workflow inference follows from the distinction between a process start and completion. Dispatching an additional task can be nearly immediate, while understanding the result, checking behavior, and deciding what to integrate may still require human attention. That gives a team a reason to inspect the handoff and completion stages when new work accumulates. The interview describes concurrent activity and includes code review as an example of what workers were already doing. It provides no repository-level counts of unfinished changes, review throughput, rework, or defects. [Haas News, third and fourth answers](https://newsroom.haas.berkeley.edu/ai-promised-to-free-up-workers-time-uc-berkeley-haas-researchers-found-the-opposite/)

### Felt momentum, cumulative strain, and expectations

Ye reports that brief sessions could feel energizing and give workers a sense of being able to do more. Some people described the overall experience differently when reflecting on work in aggregate, including feeling busier or finding it harder to disengage. Those perceptions can coexist and are useful signals at different timescales. Ye also suggests a feedback loop in which initially discretionary effort raises apparent output, a higher level of output becomes expected, and growing expectations invite more expansion. Her comments about weaker judgment and higher error risk describe possible consequences of switching and reduced recovery. The article provides neither a measured trend in expectations nor an estimate of errors or burnout. [Haas News, fourth and fifth answers](https://newsroom.haas.berkeley.edu/ai-promised-to-free-up-workers-time-uc-berkeley-haas-researchers-found-the-opposite/)

### What Ye proposes teams try

Ye's proposed “AI practice” gives organizations some control over when results and decisions demand attention. Before an important decision, she suggests making time to articulate an objection or explain the connection to an organizational goal. During ordinary execution, a team can gather low-priority results for review together and keep blocks of focused work available. She also proposes regular time for colleagues to talk through the work and reflect. The pause applies to decisions, sequencing applies to the flow of tasks and updates, and check-ins apply to coordination. These are suggested practices from the investigator. The Q&A reports no rollout or evaluation of their effects. [Haas News, sixth answer](https://newsroom.haas.berkeley.edu/ai-promised-to-free-up-workers-time-uc-berkeley-haas-researchers-found-the-opposite/)

## Extractable practitioner insights

### Use active threads and lost stopping points as diagnostic signals

For AP-04, ask what changed when opening a new thread became easier. A developer might have several agent-produced patches in flight, with each needing a review before it can be integrated. A teammate may see the same problem as a steadily growing queue of results or as interruptions arriving whenever an agent finishes. Ye's field account gives grounded reasons to look for expanded scope, work spilling between other commitments, and overlapping threads. Whether these signals amount to excessive work depends on local priorities, deadlines, and capacity to verify the results. A process waiting in the background can also be useful when the results are independent and there is capacity to use them. [SRC-0015:C03–C06, observed practice and perceived strain](https://newsroom.haas.berkeley.edu/ai-promised-to-free-up-workers-time-uc-berkeley-haas-researchers-found-the-opposite/)

### Give review and decisions a place in the workday

Ye’s advice on sequencing transfers readily to a coding team interrupted by routine agent completions. As a local adaptation, a developer with three active code changes could set a review window, park a proposed cleanup, and finish verifying the change already needed for the next release. The team could check whether fewer changes remain open at the end of the day and whether urgent issues still get timely attention. The number of active changes, the choice to park one task, and the measures are dossier examples. The interview supports considering pacing and focus time, with any effect on outcomes to be checked locally. A high-priority production incident may still need immediate review. No pattern ID has been allocated for this dossier. - **Workload and causality.** This is a qualitative case with an exploratory design. The available account describes behaviors and felt pressure. It gives no counterfactual or measured change in total work hours, output per hour, quality, or cognitive load. Case observations can guide a team’s diagnosis, including when individual tasks feel faster. Estimates of typical effects for software developers or teams would need additional evidence and defined measures. [Interview, methods, observed forms, and reflections](https://newsroom.haas.berkeley.edu/ai-promised-to-free-up-workers-time-uc-berkeley-haas-researchers-found-the-opposite/)
- **AP-04 coverage.** The strongest link is the qualitative evidence that workers took on tasks they might previously have left idle or delegated, and kept multiple threads running. How much this burdened individual developers or downstream teams, whether it harmed delivery, and what level of concurrency best balances progress and review remain open.
