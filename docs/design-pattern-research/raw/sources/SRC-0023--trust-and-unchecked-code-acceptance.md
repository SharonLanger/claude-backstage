## Reader’s orientation

The study asks what developers mean by a trustworthy suggestion, what cues they use when judging one, and how an initial decision changes during a coding session. The authors treat accepting or relying on a suggestion as an observable action and describe trustworthiness as a property of the suggestion. Someone can accept a proposal provisionally, discover a problem after integration, and revise it. That sequence makes the timing and substance of evaluation important to AP-02, which concerns accepting generated code without checking or understanding it. The paper documents unchecked acceptance in a small observational sample. Its measures stop at the session and concern individual suggestions rather than production merges or a broader style of coding. [Open preprint, §§II, III.B, VI, IX](https://cdn.amazon.science/99/78/f02aeaa049b4ba514d7f2790ade7/trust-dynamics-in-ai-assisted-development-definitions-factors-and-implications.pdf)

## Detailed analytical reading

### Three datasets answer different questions

The initial survey recruited 29 US adults familiar with Python, including 15 graduate students and 14 professionals. Twenty-two had used AI coding assistants. Respondents defined trustworthy code in their own terms and rated the importance of predefined qualities. They were also each assigned 20 short Python snippets, split evenly between original CodeXGlue snippets and GitHub Copilot versions generated from the corresponding signature and docstring. A one-line project description accompanied each snippet. Participants scored perceived trustworthiness on a 1–10 scale and explained a subset of judgments. The authors report 486 ratings and comments on a corpus of 200 snippets. Those judgments capture what people notice with limited context. Understanding the surrounding project or testing an actual integration requires more information. [Open preprint, §III.A, pp. 2–3, Table I, and §IX, p. 11](https://cdn.amazon.science/99/78/f02aeaa049b4ba514d7f2790ade7/trust-dynamics-in-ai-assisted-development-definitions-factors-and-implications.pdf#page=2)

The observational study recruited five professional developers and five student developers. All had more than three years of programming experience and regularly used LLM tools. Four chose Python projects and six chose web projects using HTML, CSS, and JavaScript. Section III.B reports six ChatGPT-only, two Copilot-only and two multi-tool participants. Table II instead lists seven ChatGPT-only, one Copilot-only and two multi-tool participants. The precise single-tool split is unresolved. The researchers recorded screen and audio while participants worked on self-selected projects via Zoom, logged prompts and actions, and asked follow-up questions about ambiguous actions. Two authors coded reasons for acceptance and rejection with the survey-derived categories and worked to agreement on modifications and deletions. The method specifies a 60-minute session and a 15-minute follow-up interview. The results describe the observation window as 60–90 minutes per session. The study reports both durations without reconciling them. [Open preprint, §III.B, pp. 3–4, Table II, and §VI.A, pp. 7–8](https://cdn.amazon.science/99/78/f02aeaa049b4ba514d7f2790ade7/trust-dynamics-in-ai-assisted-development-definitions-factors-and-implications.pdf#page=3)

The authors surveyed seven software engineering and AI researchers and eight returning survey participants about the perceived impact and practicality of four draft guidelines. They revised the guidance using the feedback. That follow-up concerns perceived usefulness and includes participants from the first phase. It supplied no intervention comparison or measured reduction in unchecked acceptance. [Open preprint, §III.C, pp. 4–5, Table III, and §VII, p. 9, Figure 5](https://cdn.amazon.science/99/78/f02aeaa049b4ba514d7f2790ade7/trust-dynamics-in-ai-assisted-development-definitions-factors-and-implications.pdf#page=4)

### What developers say they value and what they check

The survey’s prompted factor ratings placed correctness and comprehensibility first. All 29 rated correctness 4 or 5 on the five-point scale, and 25 did so for comprehensibility. The qualitative part gives correctness several practical meanings, including matching an expectation, being executable and being amenable to testing. In open-ended definitions, correctness came up most often. In explanations of snippet ratings, comprehensibility came up most often and correctness was second. Maintainability, robustness, similarity to the codebase and optimality occurred in definitions and were rarely seen during snippet assessment. Dependency, safety/security and size appeared in snippet judgments even though they did not appear in the open-ended definitions. Safety/security had also been offered as a separate prompted factor. The change in what participants mentioned tracks a change in what they could see and judge in the survey setup. [Open preprint, §§IV.A–B, pp. 4–6, Figure 1 and Table IV, and §V.B, p. 7, Figure 3](https://cdn.amazon.science/99/78/f02aeaa049b4ba514d7f2790ade7/trust-dynamics-in-ai-assisted-development-definitions-factors-and-implications.pdf#page=5)

In the live sessions, perceived functional correctness was cited in 61 of 117 initial acceptances. Nine of the ten people used testing to verify a suggestion’s correctness during their sessions. For example, OP5 tested a parser immediately before accepting it. The authors report the testing count by participant and do not give it as the fraction of suggestions checked. They also describe judgments based on a suggestion meeting expectations. The practical concern is how well the check covers what matters for the change. A first test can miss a later failure, and a snippet that provides the intended function can still alter unrelated behavior. [Open preprint, §§VI.A–B, pp. 7–8](https://cdn.amazon.science/99/78/f02aeaa049b4ba514d7f2790ade7/trust-dynamics-in-ai-assisted-development-definitions-factors-and-implications.pdf#page=8)

### What the 48% headline measures

Figure 4 counts 142 suggestions from ten sessions. The initial decisions were 117 acceptances and 25 rejections, with ignoring a suggestion counted as rejection. Of the 117 accepted suggestions, 75 were kept as originally offered and 42 were edited or removed before session end. Summing the initial rejections and subsequent edits/removals gives 67 of 142 suggestions that did not remain in their original form. The changed suggestions in this combined count may still have contributed code. [Open preprint, §VI.A, pp. 7–8, Figure 4, and §VIII.A, p. 10](https://cdn.amazon.science/99/78/f02aeaa049b4ba514d7f2790ade7/trust-dynamics-in-ai-assisted-development-definitions-factors-and-implications.pdf#page=8)

The paper labels the 67 cases as 48% and the 75 cases as 52%. Calculated from the figure, the two shares are 47.2% and 52.8% of all 142 suggestions. The sentence connecting 52% to the 117 accepted suggestions also mixes denominators. Within the 117 initial acceptances, 75/117 were kept (64.1%) and 42/117 were edited or removed (35.9%). The introduction describes 48% using mistaken trust, and the discussion uses error rate language in a comparison to human pair programming. For citation, the counts and category labels in Figure 4 define the outcome. Immediate rejection shows no initial acceptance, and edits have multiple causes. The sessions did not test defect prevalence or compare against human pair programming. [Open preprint, abstract, §I, p. 2, §VI.A, pp. 7–8, Figure 4, and §VIII.A, p. 10](https://cdn.amazon.science/99/78/f02aeaa049b4ba514d7f2790ade7/trust-dynamics-in-ai-assisted-development-definitions-factors-and-implications.pdf#page=8)

### What the researchers counted as blind acceptance

Section VI.B defines blind acceptance by a developer doing no evaluation of a suggestion before accepting it. The authors report 13 instances of this behavior followed by modifying or removing the suggestion. Eight of those 13 required modification for incorrectness. The authors separately report four instances in which blindly trusted code stayed in the codebase. The counts are at the level of suggestion events observed in these sessions. A later edit or test is compatible with the original acceptance having been blind, and code retention alone does not demonstrate that anyone checked it. [Open preprint, §VI.B(c), p. 8](https://cdn.amazon.science/99/78/f02aeaa049b4ba514d7f2790ade7/trust-dynamics-in-ai-assisted-development-definitions-factors-and-implications.pdf#page=8)

The authors give two other routes to revising an acceptance. In six cases, developers initially trusted correctness and later identified a flaw. In one example, a new application tab appeared to work after testing and later produced an error. In seven other cases, developers credited correctness and then edited or removed code because it changed something outside their request. One participant accepted generated CSS and later discovered that it changed screen sizing. The cases expose different review questions. What part of the expected behavior did the first check exercise? Which existing behavior also changed? The examples provide no basis for combining the reported cause categories into an exhaustive breakdown of all 42 edits or removals. [Open preprint, §VI.B(a–c), p. 8](https://cdn.amazon.science/99/78/f02aeaa049b4ba514d7f2790ade7/trust-dynamics-in-ai-assisted-development-definitions-factors-and-implications.pdf#page=8)

## Extractable practitioner insights

### Check the generated change when you decide to rely on it

The direct AP-02 symptom is that a developer puts a suggestion into the project before examining it. The study records later repair after unchecked acceptance as well as after an initial check with inadequate coverage. The authors’ G3 recommendation is to review generated contributions as externally authored and use appropriate unit tests before acceptance. A catalog adaptation is to treat insertion into a disposable branch as provisional, explain in one or two sentences how the diff satisfies the requested behavior, exercise a relevant case using the expected result from the task, and inspect what else changes before keeping the patch for handoff. This adapts snippet-level observations into a diff-review workflow. The authors did not evaluate this sequence as an intervention. Claims SRC-0023:C05–C08. [Open preprint, §§VI.A–B, p. 8, and VII, G3–G4, pp. 9–10](https://cdn.amazon.science/99/78/f02aeaa049b4ba514d7f2790ade7/trust-dynamics-in-ai-assisted-development-definitions-factors-and-implications.pdf#page=8)

For example, a developer asks a coding assistant to add a filter to a dashboard. Even if the filter selects the intended rows, the diff might also add a global style that changes layouts elsewhere. The task-specific check covers expected rows, including the empty result case. The scope check follows modified selectors to find affected views. The developer can revise the patch if the filter behavior works but the global style was outside the request. This example is an original application of the paper’s two review questions. Claims SRC-0023:C07–C08. [Open preprint, §VI.B(b), p. 8, and VII, G3–G4, pp. 9–10](https://cdn.amazon.science/99/78/f02aeaa049b4ba514d7f2790ade7/trust-dynamics-in-ai-assisted-development-definitions-factors-and-implications.pdf#page=8)

## Related work, conflicts, and limitations

The source gives AP-02 direct behavioral evidence at the moment a developer works with AI code suggestions. Its small and purposefully recruited sample cannot support a developer-population frequency estimate. Session retention says little about approval, deployment, security, or maintainability over time, and some edits are part of normal use of a draft. The projects and tools varied, so the observations offer no tool-specific comparison or an estimate for modern repo-wide agents. The authors discuss automation bias as a possible explanation, without a causal test. [Open preprint, §§III.B, VI, VIII.A and IX](https://cdn.amazon.science/99/78/f02aeaa049b4ba514d7f2790ade7/trust-dynamics-in-ai-assisted-development-definitions-factors-and-implications.pdf)

The Moby and Docker policy dossier provides a different point in the workflow. It documents the review and ownership expected before externally sharing AI-assisted work. It complements the interaction study as governance evidence from open-source maintainers. Neither source measures the downstream effect of enforcing a review policy. [SRC-0018, claims C02 and C08](SRC-0018--accountability-for-ai-assisted-contributions.md#claim-level-evidence-ledger)
