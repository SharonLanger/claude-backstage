# Measuring the Impact of Early-2025 AI on Experienced Open-Source Developer Productivity

## Provenance and scope read

This dossier uses the July 25, 2025 revision of the METR preprint, first submitted to arXiv on July 12. METR published a companion write-up on July 10. The paper credits Joel Becker and Nate Rush equally and lists Beth Barnes in its byline. The arXiv metadata lists her as Elizabeth Barnes. The study was conducted by METR, a nonprofit that uses developer productivity as one input to its assessment of AI research and development acceleration. Its companion write-up says the team initially expected a speedup. METR paid study participants $150 per hour and offered Cursor Pro. The companion write-up and preprint describe the same initial experiment. ## Reader's orientation

Allowing early-2025 AI tools increased estimated developer implementation time by **19%** in this field experiment, with a reported 95% confidence interval of **+2% to +39%**. The 16 participants worked on real issues in familiar, mature open-source projects. Their estimate after the experiment pointed in the opposite direction. In a smaller set of screen recordings, developers spent an observable share of their time inspecting and cleaning AI output. The study measures issue-level time under an AI-access policy. The activity labels and mixed reports of perceived effort leave the size of any causal contribution from review and the question of fatigue open. [Preprint, Fig. 1 and §§3–3.3, pp. 2 and 8–10](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.09089v2#page=2) · [Preprint, §§C.1.4 and C.2.6, pp. 20 and 23](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.09089v2#page=20) · [METR follow-up, opening estimates](https://metr.org/blog/2026-02-24-uplift-update/)

## Detailed analytical reading

### What assignment changed and what the clock counted

The authors recruited developers from their professional networks and by outreach to established open-source contributors. Participants averaged five years of experience with their own study projects. The repositories averaged about 23,000 stars and 1.1 million lines of code. The developers selected actual issues, ranging from bug reports to features and other work items, and forecast their time with and without AI before assignment. Completed tasks averaged two hours. Asking for roughly two-hour tasks or splitting larger ones kept scope manageable for the study. Work followed normal pull request and review processes. The sample and the tasks establish a setting in which a fast human baseline and repository-specific standards both mattered. [Preprint, §§1 and 2.1–2.2, pp. 2 and 4–5](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.09089v2#page=4)

Assignment was at the issue level, usually by a simulated fair coin. The authors used block randomization for 25 early issues. Excluding those issues yielded a 20% slowdown estimate. On AI-allowed issues developers chose their tools or could work without AI. The study offered Cursor Pro and a basic onboarding session. The study covered February through June 2025, with most issues completed in February and March. The predominant AI tools were Cursor Pro and Claude 3.5 or 3.7 Sonnet. Generative AI, including LLM autocomplete, was off limits in the comparison arm. Ordinary search still qualified. A developer could work on several assigned issues and choose the order. This setup estimates the effect of permitting AI as people actually chose to use it. The effect of mandatory AI use, of a specific tool or model, or of a prescribed prompting workflow would require a different comparison. [Preprint, §§2.2–2.2.1, pp. 5–6](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.09089v2#page=5) · [Preprint, §C.3.6, p. 28](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.09089v2#page=28) · [Preprint, §E.3, p. 33](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.09089v2#page=33)

Total implementation time combined the author’s initial work through PR submission with their subsequent work responding to review and preparing the PR to merge. The instructions asked for the contributor’s active time and explicitly excluded days waiting for a human reviewer. A PR submitted on Monday and revised on Thursday could therefore contribute 20 minutes of post-review implementation if that was the developer’s actual rework. The outcome supplies no time for the other human reviewer and no measure of team throughput or calendar lead time. For 27 issues without a recorded post-review outcome by study close, the main analysis imputed each assignment arm’s mean post-review time. [Preprint, §2.3, p. 6](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.09089v2#page=6) · [Preprint, §C.3.4, p. 27](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.09089v2#page=27) · [Preprint, §G.2.1, Time Tracking, p. 41](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.09089v2#page=41)

### Interpreting the time estimate and the forecasts

Of the 246 completed issues, 136 allowed AI and 110 disallowed it. The authors fit a log-time regression with an AI-allowance indicator and the log of the developer’s pre-assignment forecast of time without AI. They report the exponentiated assignment coefficient minus one as the proportional change in time. The forecast serves as a difficulty proxy. AI-allowed issues happened to have higher forecasts of time without AI, a difference the authors report as statistically insignificant. The unadjusted comparison of mean times was 34% longer with AI allowed. The regression estimate of 19% accounts for the forecast difference. The authors’ default interval uses HC3 robust standard errors. Developer-clustered alternatives yielded similar results, while resampling developers and then their issues gave a wider interval. Six AI-allowed and seven AI-disallowed issues went unfinished in their issue dropout check. Restricting analysis to developers who completed every assigned issue in that check gave a 21% slowdown. This sensitivity check addresses one way selective completion could influence the estimate. [Preprint, Fig. 1, §§3–3.1, pp. 2 and 8–9](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.09089v2#page=8) · [Preprint, §§C.3.3–C.3.5, pp. 26–27](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.09089v2#page=26) · [Preprint, §§D.1–D.3, pp. 28–29](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.09089v2#page=28) · [METR follow-up, opening estimates](https://metr.org/blog/2026-02-24-uplift-update/) Appendix C.3.3 (p. 26) separately records 24 issues deliberately dropped, for reasons such as difficulty or superseding work. The authors report finding no link between the recorded reasons and treatment assignment.

The initial developer forecasts implied 24% shorter time with AI. After the study, the developers’ retrospective estimates still implied 20% shorter time. The retrospective numbers answer a counterfactual question about how work would have gone under a different policy, whereas the primary time logs report work on the assigned issues. The logged times have their own limitations. As one check, the authors estimated a 25% slowdown from total durations of available screen recordings, and a 24% slowdown from self-reported times on the same subset. This comparison addresses one possible arm-level difference in self-reporting on recorded work. Short, unrecorded changes and work outside recording sessions could still affect measurement. Alternative post-review imputations assigning zero in one arm and an hour in the other yielded estimates of 14% and 23% longer in the AI-allowed arm. These are sensitivity estimates, separate from the headline confidence interval. [Preprint, Fig. 1 and §3.1, pp. 2 and 8–9](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.09089v2#page=8) · [Preprint, §C.3.4, p. 27](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.09089v2#page=27) · [Preprint, §G.8, p. 45](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.09089v2#page=45)

### What the screen recordings and interviews show about review

The researchers manually labeled 128 recordings covering 143 hours. The activity analysis retained 74 recordings covering 84 hours after excluding detected condition violations, recordings broken for more than 10% of their duration, and those differing by more than 20% from the contributor’s reported time. Labeling distinguished 27 activities at about ten-second resolution. The researchers prioritized shorter videos, and developers often skipped recording minor post-review changes. These decisions limit how confidently the labeled minutes generalize to the full set of issues. [Preprint, §§2.4 and 3.2, pp. 8–9](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.09089v2#page=8) · [Preprint, §G.8, pp. 45–50](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.09089v2#page=45)

The AI-allowed arm spent a smaller share of recorded time directly coding and reading or searching. Prompting, waiting for generations and reviewing output became visible categories of work. In the 44 valid AI-allowed recordings singled out in Appendix C, about 9% of time went to reviewing and cleaning AI outputs and about 4% to waiting for generation. Figure 6 reports shares, Figures 19 and 20 report mean minutes, and Figure 21 breaks the shares down by finer categories. The labeling guide distinguishes reading a generation, editing retained AI code, abandoning a suggestion and waiting. It gives testing and debugging their own labels. A developer checking a generated patch with tests can therefore do verification that is outside the narrow inspection and cleanup figure. Coding time and review time also compete for some of the same implementation minutes. [Preprint, §3.2 and Fig. 6, pp. 9–10](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.09089v2#page=10) · [Preprint, §C.1.4, p. 20](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.09089v2#page=20) · [Preprint, §C.2.9, p. 25](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.09089v2#page=25) · [Preprint, §E.4 and Figs. 19–21, pp. 33–34](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.09089v2#page=33) · [Preprint, §G.8, pp. 48–50](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.09089v2#page=48)

Cursor analytics, available for 13 developers, showed acceptance of fewer than 44% of generations as reported in Appendix C. Acceptance is a behavior, with convenience and partial utility among the possible reasons for keeping a suggestion. Exit evidence reported that 75% of respondents typically read every line of AI code used in their implementation and 56% usually needed substantial edits to the code they used (Appendix G.5.1, exit survey items 12–13, p. 45). The authors describe people trying another prompt or reverting after they had already spent time assessing, testing or editing a suggestion. They also describe cleanup on proposals developers retained. Examples include reconciling style and repository conventions or noticing an implicit compatibility requirement. These examples help explain why a patch that arrives quickly can still take time to ship. [Preprint, §2.4, p. 7](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.09089v2#page=7) · [Preprint, §§C.1.3–C.1.5, pp. 19–20](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.09089v2#page=19)

The paper treats low reliability, tacit repository knowledge, repository complexity, developer familiarity and overoptimism as plausible contributors. Its mechanism analysis combines descriptive screens, interviews and exploratory subgroups, with limited statistical power for multiple comparisons. AI permission was randomized. Time spent reviewing, patch reliability and the choice to keep, revise or discard a suggestion evolved after assignment and were not independently manipulated. Review minutes can catch failures and can coexist with savings in other work. Dividing the 9% activity share by the 19% estimated change in total time would mix different populations and denominators and would not identify causal mediation. Separately, post-human-review rework averaged 15 minutes in AI-allowed issues and 9 minutes in AI-disallowed issues. The authors report that this difference was statistically insignificant and call the effect of initially low-quality PRs unclear. Their qualitative assessment of PR quality gives no independent defect-rate or reviewer-time estimate. [Preprint, §3.3 and Table 1, pp. 10–11](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.09089v2#page=10) · [Preprint, §§C.1 and C.2.7, pp. 18–20 and 23](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.09089v2#page=23)

The protocol asked developers to rate perceived effort on a five-point issue scale. Appendix C presents mixed qualitative reports on how effortful AI-assisted work felt, including work that felt easier and work that felt about the same. These are two further reasons to keep observed review time and cognitive strain distinct. Fatigue would call for evidence such as a change in alertness or review accuracy over time, which this paper did not measure. A bottleneck in a team review queue would require separate evidence on arrivals, pending changes and reviewer capacity. Neither follows from a share of one contributor’s recorded implementation time. [Preprint, §C.2.6, p. 23](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.09089v2#page=23) · [Preprint, §G.2.1, Perceived Effort, pp. 41–42](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.09089v2#page=41)

### Late-2025 data change what can be carried forward

In the update linked from its original write-up, METR reports that a second experiment starting in August 2025 covered 57 developers, 143 repositories and more than 800 tasks. Ten developers returned from the original study and 47 were newly recruited from a wider pool. On the signed change-in-time scale, the returning group’s later point estimate was **−18%** (reported interval −38% to +9%) and the new group’s estimate was **−4%** (−15% to +9%). Both intervals include no change. The later point estimates suggest shorter implementation time for those groups’ observed work, with considerable uncertainty. Changes in participating developers, projects, selection and workflows prevent a clean estimate of how much improvement came from model or tool advances. [METR original write-up, update link](https://metr.org/blog/2025-07-10-early-2025-ai-experienced-os-dev-study/) · [METR follow-up, opening estimates and Details of the productivity study](https://metr.org/blog/2026-02-24-uplift-update/#details-of-the-productivity-study)

The later experiment again randomized issues only after developers submitted them. METR says some people declined to participate or stay because they would have to work without AI on some issues. Between 30% and 50% of surveyed developers said that they withheld some tasks for that reason. That percentage concerns surveyed developers, not the share of tasks withheld. Pay also fell from $150 to $50 per hour, which METR says likely affected who participated. Assignment could affect completion as well. One developer finished none of their AI-disallowed tasks. Some developers also reported changing the kinds of work they attempted or the amount of tests and documentation they produced. For people running agents concurrently and moving to other tasks while agents worked, per-issue time became harder to report. [METR follow-up, Wider adoption of AI has made it more difficult to measure task-level productivity](https://metr.org/blog/2026-02-24-uplift-update/#wider-adoption-of-ai-has-made-it-more-difficult-to-measure-task-level-productivity)

METR interprets the recruitment and task selection as likely to miss developers and work with high expected gains. This is its assessment of bias direction. How much the omitted developers and tasks actually benefited is unknown. Even if the selection argument is correct, the update supplies no statistical lower bound on their speedups. The newer data add a different tool period and a larger participant pool, with partial participant overlap and shared research ownership and design. They are comparative context from a related METR experiment. They provide no fresh measure of review fatigue, reviewer demand or the fraction of time changes caused by review. [METR follow-up, opening discussion and design changes](https://metr.org/blog/2026-02-24-uplift-update/)

## Extractable practitioner insights

### Count the work after the first patch

For OB-11, the strongest use of this source is diagnosing a gap between perceived speed and time to a reviewable change. A catalog adaptation is to decide what counts as ready before starting, then record hands-on time through checking, cleanup, testing and revisions after PR feedback. A developer comparing an AI-assisted fix with similar manual work could keep a separate record of elapsed time and reviewer time if those are the practical concerns. For example, once the first patch for a familiar bug is on screen, the developer still needs to inspect callers, check a compatibility case and run the repository's normal checks. Fast generation answers only one part of the comparison. The specific logging routine and example are catalog adaptations. The study compared tool-allowance regimes and motivates measurement before assigning a speedup to a workflow. Claims SRC-0011:C03–C08. [Preprint, §§2.3, 3.1 and 3.2, pp. 6 and 8–10](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.09089v2#page=6)

### Tie the scope of each change to what can be verified

For BP-05, the trial motivates exposing the work needed to inspect and clean a generated diff. It asked participants to submit manageable issues, and it offers anecdotes about larger attempted changes touching unexpected code. It did not compare slice sizes or compare vertical and structural decomposition. A catalog adaptation is to choose one observable behavior, define how to check it, and inspect that diff before another request widens it. The distinct [AI Hero dossier on vertical slices](SRC-0006--vertical-slices-for-agent-tickets.md) describes the actual decomposition technique. These sources can support the rationale and procedure from different evidence types, with a remaining evaluation gap for the effect of slicing on fatigue or throughput. Claims SRC-0011:C01, C08–C11. [Preprint, §2.2, p. 5](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.09089v2#page=5) · [Preprint, §§C.1.3–C.1.5, pp. 19–20](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.09089v2#page=19)

### Notice when verifying a candidate keeps adding work

For AP-01, the observed retries and reversions provide examples of time spent on a candidate that ultimately could not be used. A catalog adaptation is to check early whether the current patch meets one decisive repository constraint. If a branch still touches unrelated modules after the developer narrows the request, they can compare the remaining work of repairing the branch with returning to known-good code and implementing the smaller change. The observed cases inform that choice. The study did not assign stop rules, compare resets with continued prompting, or estimate a useful number of attempts. Claims SRC-0011:C09 and C11. [Preprint, §§C.1.1 and C.1.3–C.1.4, pp. 18–20](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.09089v2#page=18)

## Related work, conflicts, and limitations

Appendix B compares the early trial with studies that assign a synthetic task or count code, commits or PRs in ongoing projects. Those studies can disagree numerically while measuring different work and outcomes. The early trial illustrates the cost of allowing AI on specific, prespecified issues with established contribution standards. It leaves longer-term learning, team output value, and speed on new projects open. Drafts, pull requests merged and maintainers’ elapsed review time each need their own interpretation. METR’s later experiment is the closest comparison discussed here, with changed tools, partial sample overlap, selection and timing problems. Its direction gives reason to treat 19% as a setting- and period-specific estimate. [Preprint, §4.1, p. 12 and Appendix B, pp. 16–17](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.09089v2#page=16) · [METR follow-up](https://metr.org/blog/2026-02-24-uplift-update/)

For OB-11, the source offers direct evidence of review and cleanup as visible work, a randomized estimate of overall task time in one setting, and suggestive evidence about mechanisms. The effect of output volume on human fatigue, how review accuracy changes during a session, and whether team review queues grow remain open. BP-05 receives supporting motivation for reviewable scope and AP-01 receives examples of sunk time. Effect estimates for those specific practices would need a comparison that assigns or otherwise identifies a change in those practices. [Preprint, §§3.2–3.3, pp. 9–11](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.09089v2#page=9) · [Preprint, §G.2.1, pp. 41–42](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.09089v2#page=41)
